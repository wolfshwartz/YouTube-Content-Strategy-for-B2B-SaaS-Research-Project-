# Playbook: YouTube Content Strategy for B2B SaaS

An operating procedure for a B2B SaaS company starting or restarting a YouTube channel, built from 12 video transcripts and 6 LinkedIn posts across 10 practitioners. Full source list in [`research/sources.md`](research/sources.md).

**Who this is for:** a marketing team of one to five people at a B2B SaaS company with a self-serve or sales-assisted motion. It is not written for enterprise ABM-led companies with 12-month sales cycles, and I explain why in [Weaknesses](#weaknesses-of-this-playbook).

**How to read the citations:** every recommendation carries its source and date. Where sources disagree, the recommendation says so and points to [Where experts disagree](#where-experts-disagree). Where I am extending past what a source actually said, it is marked **[my inference]**.

---

> # ⚠️ Read this before anything else
>
> ## This is a long game, and it has to be built in sequence
>
> **Nothing in this playbook works if you try to do all of it at once.** The phases below are written in order on purpose. Each one assumes the previous one is already running.
>
> **On the time horizon.** Kyle Denhoff, who runs this for a living at HubSpot, is unambiguous: none of these strategies produce results in 30 days, and you are building a brand for the next three years. He compares it to paying into a retirement account rather than running a campaign, and says plainly that if you need leads inside 30 days, the answer is paid media, not content (source: Kyle Denhoff, "Practical Ideas to Improve Your Content Strategy", Haley Marketing, YouTube, 11.11.2024 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TN7NQL8t-GI). The commitment thresholds the sources give before you are even entitled to judge results: 90 days at one video per week (source: Greg Miroslaw, YouTube, 18.05.2026 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gZZ5D1bceMA), or three months at one video every two weeks (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026 — https://youtu.be/6TBAHC7ksbU). Marlon Doll's version: success comes from a library that compounds week after week, not from one video (source: Marlon Doll, YouTube, 10.12.2025 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OBK5DZJZnjs).
>
> **On sequence.** Kyle Denhoff's own onboarding advice is to reorient around a *single* strategy and a *single* series, then give it time to mature. He is blunt that formats are hard, and recommends spending a month simply getting one format right before worrying about anything else (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026). One platform, one channel, one format, one cadence. Everything else is a later problem.
>
> **A workable order for the first 90 days [my inference, sequencing the phases below]:**
>
> | When | Do this | Do **not** do this yet |
> |---|---|---|
> | Weeks 1 to 2 | [Phase 0](#phase-0-decide-what-the-channel-is-for-before-you-film-anything) decisions and channel audit. No filming at all. | Buying equipment, designing thumbnails |
> | Weeks 3 to 4 | [Phase 1](#phase-1-research-find-the-topics-before-you-script) research, build a topic backlog | Filming before the backlog exists |
> | Month 2 | Ship 4 to 6 bottom-of-funnel videos in **one** format (see [Disagreement 2](#disagreement-2-where-to-start-in-the-funnel)) | Adding a second format or channel |
> | Month 3 | Hold cadence. Refine the one format. | Repurposing waterfalls, paid creator deals |
> | Month 4+ | Only now widen toward the 40/30/30 mix ([2.1](#phase-2-content-architecture-the-three-layers)) | Judging the channel dead before your threshold |
>
> **The failure this prevents** is the most common one in the entire research set: a company launches a channel, a second channel, a repurposing pipeline, and three content formats simultaneously, exhausts itself by video eight, and concludes YouTube does not work for B2B. It was never given the chance to.
>
> **One caution against the opposite error.** "Be patient" is not a licence to spend indefinitely. Patience is only defensible when paired with the exit conditions in [Original idea 2](#original-idea-2-decide-what-failure-looks-like-before-you-publish-video-one): decide *before* you publish video one what result would tell you the format is wrong, so that "give it time" never becomes an argument you can never lose.

## Phase 0. Decide what the channel is for, before you film anything

**0.1 — Concentrate your output instead of spreading it.** There are two separate decisions hiding in this one, and they are easier to get right when kept apart.

*Which platform.* Pick the one platform where your Ideal Customer Profile (ICP) already spends time and where you can still earn organic reach, then commit to it rather than maintaining a thin presence everywhere (source: TK Kader, "If I Started in 2026, Here's My B2B SaaS Content Strategy for $1M ARR", YouTube, 18.05.2025 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YxAd96vLzCc). Kyle Denhoff sets the same ceiling from the other direction: trying to be everywhere is unsustainable, so narrow to the two or three places your buyers actually are and build the strategy around those (source: Kyle Denhoff, "Practical Ideas to Improve Your Content Strategy", Haley Marketing, YouTube, 11.11.2024 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TN7NQL8t-GI).

*How many YouTube channels, once you have chosen YouTube.* One. This needs saying plainly because HubSpot runs about 13 and that number gets cited as a structure worth copying. Asked directly whether a newcomer should start with a single channel, Kyle Denhoff answered yes without hesitation, and immediately qualified HubSpot's own position: they are a large company with resources, they have been running YouTube for over five years, and they arrived at 13 channels through what he describes as a lot of testing and a lot of failing (source: Kyle Denhoff, "Ep 38: The YouTube B2B Practitioner's Playbook", Weidert Group, YouTube, 23.06.2026 — https://youtu.be/6TBAHC7ksbU). **HubSpot's 13 channels are an end state they earned, not a starting structure.** Reading their current setup as a template inverts the order in which it was built.

**Why this matters more than it sounds [my inference].** YouTube rewards consistency of talent, format, and release schedule (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026), and all three are properties of an individual channel, not of your company. So splitting a fixed production capacity across two channels does not give you two growing channels. It gives you two channels each publishing at half the cadence, each with a weaker consistency signal and a thinner library for the algorithm to recommend from. With identical resources, one channel publishing weekly beats two publishing fortnightly. Add a second channel only when the first is already at your target cadence and you are adding capacity, not dividing it.

**0.2 — Declare the channel a demand channel or a brand channel.** Decide whether you are capturing demand that already exists on YouTube, or generating awareness as a category thought leader. Set a reach goal (monthly views) and a demand goal (content leads converting on site) (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026 — https://youtu.be/6TBAHC7ksbU).

**0.3 — Audit what you already have.** Before planning anything new, look at views, average view duration, and new subscribers on your existing videos to see what has worked and what has not (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026 — https://youtu.be/6TBAHC7ksbU).

**0.4 — Define the viewer, not the buyer persona.** Ask who you are trying to reach, what problems they have, and what videos they already watch, including competitors' (source: Marlon Doll, "How to Grow your SaaS YouTube Channel", YouTube, 10.12.2025 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OBK5DZJZnjs).

> ### What Phase 0 is protecting you from
>
> **The failure:** a channel that accumulates uploads without ever building an audience. Videos keep going up, the view count stays flat, and nobody can say why.
>
> **Why it happens:** when nobody has decided what the channel is for, each video ends up serving whoever inside the company requested it. A webinar recording because sales wanted it hosted somewhere. A product launch because the release needed a video. A conference talk because it existed. Every upload is individually defensible and none of them connect.
>
> **Why that is specifically fatal on YouTube:** both the algorithm and the returning viewer need to be able to predict what your channel is about. Neither can, when consecutive uploads have nothing to do with each other. You are not building a library that compounds, you are building an archive.
>
> **Two named cases.** GitHub had multiple departments each producing content on their own agenda, disconnected from any overall strategy, and the channel flatlined until it was rebuilt around what their audience was actually searching for (source: Marlon Doll, "How to Grow your SaaS YouTube Channel", YouTube, 10.12.2025 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OBK5DZJZnjs). Greg Miroslaw's Lemlist-versus-Instantly comparison is closer to a controlled test: two cold email tools, same category, same buyers, but Lemlist posts what he calls random content with no strategy and no packaging, down to a channel link that invites you to subscribe to the channel you are already on, while Instantly's channel became one of the biggest drivers of their growth (source: Greg Miroslaw, "How To Get SaaS Clients From YouTube in 2026", YouTube, 18.05.2026 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gZZ5D1bceMA).
>
> **The point of putting those two side by side:** the gap between Lemlist's channel and Instantly's is not budget, product quality, or market. Both companies sell comparable software to the same buyers. The difference is that one of them made these four decisions before filming started and the other did not.

## Phase 1. Research: find the topics before you script

**1.1 — Treat YouTube as a search engine.** For most B2B SaaS companies the highest-return move is to find the specific keywords your ICP already searches and rank for them (source: Greg Miroslaw, YouTube, 18.05.2026). Starting out, target smaller-volume, lower-competition keywords rather than the biggest terms in your space (source: Greg Miroslaw, YouTube, 18.05.2026; consistent with Sam Oh, "YouTube SEO: How to Rank YouTube Videos #1", YouTube, 27.07.2022 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Nh0mDbfEdVA, who advises checking the top three results for channel size and topical authority and moving on if you would be outmatched).

**1.2 — Target search hits over viral hits.** A viral hit spikes and dies once the algorithm exhausts the interested audience; a search hit produces consistent traffic from both Google and YouTube month after month (source: Sam Oh, "YouTube SEO for AI Search", Ahrefs AEO course, YouTube, 13.05.2026 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_b_cTpLHMTM).

**1.3 — Mine adjacent niches for formats, not just your own.** Search your niche, then niches whose viewers share demographics, psychographics, and viewing behaviour. Formats proven elsewhere but absent from your niche are the opportunity (source: Jake Trinder, "Use This Algorithm, It'll Blow Up Your YouTube Channel", YouTube, 28.06.2026 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LOgDwuUsmzw). Jake's named proof: the "watch me start and sell a service in 10 hours" video for Instantly, at ~440,000 views, was adapted from a digital-product video in an unrelated niche.

**1.4 — Verify an "outlier" before you copy it.** When a video looks like a breakout, check the comment count against the view count and check the view graph. A spike with disproportionately low engagement suggests external traffic was pushed to it, not that the algorithm and audience actually rewarded it (source: Jake Trinder, YouTube, 28.06.2026). **This is the single most useful methodological point in the entire source set,** because every other researcher in this group treats high view counts as evidence without testing for it.

**1.5 — Use your sales calls as a research input.** Content research is not only keyword research: review sales calls and talk to customers (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026). I extend this substantially in [My original ideas](#my-original-ideas).

**1.6 — Treat keyword tool volumes as estimates, not facts.** Sam Oh pulled his own ranking data for 14 keywords his channel ranked #1 for and back-calculated implied click-through rates: TubeBuddy's volumes implied a 0.2% average CTR to a top-ranking video and vidIQ's implied 0.63%, against a realistic 15 to 18% (source: Sam Oh, YouTube, 27.07.2022). His conclusion, which I adopt: no keyword tool is accurate; use volumes for relative comparison only.

## Phase 2. Content architecture: the three layers

All four SaaS-specific sources converge on a three-layer funnel with different names. Use this as the content mix.

| Layer | Purpose | What it looks like | Named by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Top of funnel** | Reach your ICP before they are searching for you | Broad educational videos that never mention your product in the title | Samu Kovács (40%), Greg Miroslaw, Marlon Doll ("authority-building") |
| **Middle of funnel** | Build trust, connect problem to product | Category comparisons, case studies, client interviews, strategy breakdowns, teardowns | Samu Kovács (30%), Greg Miroslaw ("nurturing"), Marlon Doll ("bridge") |
| **Bottom of funnel** | Convert people already evaluating you | Product tutorials, demos, feature comparisons, competitor comparisons | Samu Kovács (30%), Greg Miroslaw, Marlon Doll ("conversion"), Kyle Denhoff |

**2.1 — Use a 40/30/30 split as your default mix.** 40% top of funnel, 30% middle, 30% bottom (source: Samu Kovács, "I Built a $320M/yr SaaS YouTube Strategy (LIVE)", YouTube, 29.06.2026 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l1S2Y25kjhs). This is the only explicit ratio anyone in the set gives, which is a reason to hold it loosely.

**2.2 — Do not put your product name in top-of-funnel titles.** Samu Kovács's Calendly teardown found every video title on Calendly's channel contained the word "Calendly", capturing only the tip of the demand iceberg while topics like "how to book sales calls" and "best scheduling platform" went unserved (source: Samu Kovács, YouTube, 29.06.2026). Greg Miroslaw states the same failure directly: SaaS companies post about product updates and new features, and "nobody goes to YouTube and types in 'Instantly product update'" (source: Greg Miroslaw, YouTube, 18.05.2026).

**2.3 — Still own your branded search terms at the bottom of the funnel.** If people searching "[your product] tutorial" find third-party channels instead of yours, you have ceded your own branded results (source: Samu Kovács, YouTube, 29.06.2026). Kyle Denhoff frames product tutorials as the foundation layer, because buyers want to self-educate fully before ever speaking to sales (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026).

**2.4 — Commit to one repeatable format before adding a second.** YouTube treats channels like TV shows and rewards consistency in talent, format, and release schedule; inconsistent formats break the reason to come back (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026). Organise videos into playlists by theme, which serves both the algorithm and returning viewers (same source).

**2.5 — Restrict upload access to one person.** Asked how to handle many people with upload permissions, Kyle Denhoff's answer was to remove their access; one person owns packaging and uploading because it is critical to channel performance (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026).

## Phase 3. Packaging: title and thumbnail

**3.1 — Write the title and thumbnail first, before the script.** HubSpot's video briefs start with title, thumbnail, and description because those determine whether the video is discovered at all (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026).

**3.2 — Produce five to seven thumbnail variations and split-test.** HubSpot produces five to seven variations testing face, headline, wording, and colour, and found yellow correlates with higher click-through (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026). Marlon Doll independently recommends split-testing thumbnails and warns that brand managers who refuse to step outside brand guidelines sacrifice performance for consistency (source: Marlon Doll, YouTube, 10.12.2025).

**3.3 — The thumbnail's job is to stop the scroll, not to explain the video.** Samu Kovács describes the viewer sequence as thumbnail catches the eye, title is read, thumbnail is checked again, then click. His Calendly critique: their existing thumbnails "tell rather than show", carry too much text, and have no visual anchor (source: Samu Kovács, YouTube, 29.06.2026). Marlon Doll frames the same pairing as the title calling back to the thumbnail to create a curiosity gap (source: Marlon Doll, YouTube, 10.12.2025).

**3.4 — Title strategy depends on where the traffic comes from.** This is contested. See [Disagreement 1](#disagreement-1-keyword-first-titles-vs-editorial-hooks).

## Phase 4. Scripting

**4.1 — Script educational content rather than improvising.** Unscripted tangents cost retention and average view duration (source: Sam Oh, YouTube, 27.07.2022).

**4.2 — Build the hook around who, what, and why.** Who the video is for, what they will get, and why you are qualified, kept concise (source: Samu Kovács, YouTube, 29.06.2026). Ed Lawrence gives a near-identical three-part intro, adding an explicit time budget of under 15 seconds (source: Ed Lawrence, "Copy This YouTube Strategy, It'll Blow Up Your Business", YouTube, 16.04.2025 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7L289H87gIU). Two independent sources converging on the same structure is the strongest evidence in this playbook; use the merged version, with Ed's 15-second cap.

**4.3 — Front-load your single best insight immediately after the intro.** Getting viewers past the first third of the video makes them much more likely to finish, and the end is where conversion happens (source: Ed Lawrence, YouTube, 16.04.2025). Marlon Doll's version is to hook in the first 30 seconds and add narrative beats, a case study, a stat, or a laugh, to reset attention (source: Marlon Doll, YouTube, 10.12.2025).

**4.4 — Make familiar information feel new.** Ed Lawrence's worked example: rather than repeating "improve your sales page conversion", he built a spreadsheet letting viewers input their own view counts and see the revenue implications, which doubled as a lead magnet (source: Ed Lawrence, YouTube, 16.04.2025).

**4.5 — Say your target keyword out loud in the video.** Google extracts and understands audio, and YouTube auto-generates the transcript from it, so the keyword needs to be spoken, not only written in metadata (source: Sam Oh, YouTube, 13.05.2026, citing Google's VP of Search; consistent with Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026).

**4.6 — Video length: no consensus.** See [Disagreement 3](#disagreement-3-video-length).

## Phase 5. Conversion: turning views into pipeline

**5.1 — Ask for the click within the first 90 seconds.** Ed Lawrence compared two of his own videos: the one with fewer total views produced significantly more link clicks and leads, the difference being that he asked viewers to sign up inside the first 90 seconds (source: Ed Lawrence, YouTube, 16.04.2025).

**5.2 — Repeat the ask three times: first 90 seconds, middle, and end.** The end reminder alone was the main difference in lead volume between two of his videos, and the mid-roll ask can be an on-screen text overlay rather than an interruption (source: Ed Lawrence, YouTube, 16.04.2025).

**5.3 — Offer one substantial asset, consistently, across every video.** TK Kader calls this a manifesto: a single document articulating what you do, why, and why you are best at it, linked from every post, converting engagement into an identifiable lead rather than a like (source: TK Kader, YouTube, 18.05.2025, and LinkedIn, 18.05.2025 — https://www.linkedin.com/posts/tkkader_if-i-started-saas-in-2025-heres-my-b2b-share-7329902612735033344-AjnT/). His framing, which I think is the correct prioritisation: one lead that becomes a customer is worth more than a thousand likes that lead nowhere.

**5.4 — Keep the product mention short and natural.** Making a video feel like an ad is the fastest way to kill its performance, because people are there for value, not to be sold to (source: Samu Kovács, YouTube, 29.06.2026). Greg Miroslaw's example of this done well is a client case-study video where the product appears naturally partway through rather than being the premise (source: Greg Miroslaw, YouTube, 18.05.2026).

**5.5 — Point end screens at your highest-converting video, not your newest.** Once you know which video converts best, route engaged viewers from every other video to it (source: Ed Lawrence, YouTube, 16.04.2025). Sam Oh's parallel reasoning is that end screens extend session watch time, which is what YouTube optimises for (source: Sam Oh, YouTube, 27.07.2022).

**5.6 — Retarget viewers off-platform.** Do not treat YouTube as the end of the funnel; retarget viewers with a follow-up demo or comparison video to move warm leads toward a decision (source: Marlon Doll, YouTube, 10.12.2025).

## Phase 6. Publish and optimise

**6.1 — Optimisation checklist.** Video filename set to the target keyword; keyword in the title; title under ~60 characters to avoid truncation; description as a genuine summary with the keyword in the first lines; timestamps, which become chapters and can surface separately in Google; closed captions uploaded; cards and end screens added (source: Sam Oh, YouTube, 27.07.2022 and 13.05.2026). Sam Oh rates tags as low-value but cheap to fill in.

**6.2 — Match the format already ranking for your keyword.** If tutorials dominate the results, make a tutorial; if listicles rank, make a listicle. You are matching searcher intent, not competing on originality of format (source: Sam Oh, YouTube, 13.05.2026 and 27.07.2022).

**6.3 — Leave comments enabled.** Comments are an engagement signal to the algorithm. Disabling them is a brand-protection decision that costs you reach, and is a judgement call rather than a default (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026).

**6.4 — Optimise for AI answer engines, not only YouTube search.** YouTube is the most-cited domain in Google's AI Overviews, and Ahrefs' data puts the correlation between YouTube mentions and ChatGPT visibility at 0.737, described as the strongest single factor they studied (source: Sam Oh, YouTube, 13.05.2026). Kyle Denhoff reports the same shift from the buyer side and expects B2B buyers to increasingly ask LLMs about pricing, features, and case studies, which argues for tutorial and feature-comparison content (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026).

> **Skepticism note.** A 0.737 correlation is a correlation, published by a company selling the tool that measures it, and it does not establish that publishing more YouTube videos causes AI visibility. Large well-known brands plausibly have both more YouTube presence and more AI visibility for the same underlying reason. I have kept the recommendation because the underlying mechanics (transcripts are indexed, LLMs cite YouTube) are independently confirmed by Kyle Denhoff's buyer-side account, but treat the specific number as marketing, not as evidence.

## Phase 7. Measure, and know when to stop

**7.1 — Track three quality signals day to day: thumbnail click-through rate, average view duration, and engagement (likes and comments).** These are the signals that determine whether the algorithm expands distribution. Views and subscribers are reviewed monthly or quarterly, not daily (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026).

**7.2 — Watch first-24-hour and first-7-day view velocity relative to your own channel average,** as an early quality signal (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026).

**7.3 — Publish at minimum every two weeks; weekly is better.** Kyle Denhoff's floor is one video every two weeks, sustained for three months before judging (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026). Greg Miroslaw's version is one video per week for at least 90 days before judging results, with batch recording to make that survivable for a founder (source: Greg Miroslaw, YouTube, 18.05.2026).

**7.4 — Expect a multi-month horizon, and set expectations upward before you start.** Samu Kovács's projection for a channel executing this well: months 1 to 2 improve leading metrics, months 3 to 4 produce the first breakout videos at 5,000 to 10,000+ organic views, months 5 to 8 reach consistent lead generation (source: Samu Kovács, YouTube, 29.06.2026). Kyle Denhoff frames content as a three-year brand investment closer to a retirement account than a campaign, and says explicitly that if you need leads in 30 days you need paid media instead (source: Kyle Denhoff, "Practical Ideas to Improve Your Content Strategy", Haley Marketing, YouTube, 11.11.2024 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TN7NQL8t-GI).

> **Note on Samu's projection:** these are the forecast numbers in a public teardown by an agency owner pitching a prospective client, not audited results. Treat the shape of the curve as useful and the specific figures as optimistic.

**7.5 — Define kill criteria before you start.** No source does this. See [My original ideas](#my-original-ideas).

## Where experts disagree

### Disagreement 1: Keyword-first titles vs. editorial hooks

**Sam Oh recommends** putting the search keyword directly in the title and keeping creativity out of it: "This isn't the place for clever or click-baity titles. If the keyword is 'how to use Google Docs', that should be right in your title. Save the creativity for the thumbnail" (source: Sam Oh, YouTube, 13.05.2026).

**Kyle Denhoff recommends** layering an editorial hook over the keyword, and describes this as a deliberate move away from how HubSpot used to do it. His worked example: for the keyword "LinkedIn content strategy", the old approach was "Top 10 LinkedIn content strategies"; the new approach is to find the company doing it best and title it around the outcome, such as the LinkedIn content strategy that drove $10 million in revenue (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026).

**Where I land: Kyle Denhoff, except in one specific case where Sam Oh is right.**

These two are not really giving opposite advice. They are describing two different situations, and neither of them says so. One question settles it:

> **How did this viewer find the video? Did they go looking for it, or did it appear in front of them?**

Those two viewers need completely different titles.

**Situation 1: they searched for it.** Someone typed a query into YouTube or Google and your video came back. This person already knows what they want. Your title's only job is to obviously match what they typed. Cleverness actively hurts here, because a clever title looks less like the answer they just asked for.

For a scheduling product, someone searching `calendly tutorial` should find a title like **"How to Use Calendly in 2026: Step-by-Step Tutorial"**. Plain and literal, on purpose. **Sam Oh is right for this case,** which in this playbook means your bottom-of-funnel videos: product tutorials, demos, and "[product A] vs [product B]" comparisons ([Phase 2](#phase-2-content-architecture-the-three-layers)).

**Situation 2: it appeared in front of them.** They were scrolling the YouTube home page, or it showed up in the sidebar beside a video they were already watching. This person was not looking for you and did not ask for anything. Your title is competing against roughly 20 other thumbnails for a click they had no intention of giving, so a plain keyword title gives them no reason to pick yours.

Samu Kovács's suggested title for Calendly, **"6 Tools Every Small Business Needs in 2026"**, is this kind of title. Nobody searches that phrase. It has to earn the click by itself. **Kyle Denhoff is right here,** which covers your top and middle-of-funnel videos.

**The resulting rule:** plain keyword title when they searched for it, editorial hook when the title has to earn the click.

**Two further reasons I lean toward Kyle overall, where the two do genuinely overlap:**

1. **Age of the advice.** Sam Oh's core SEO video is from **July 2022**, roughly four years before I am writing this. It predates both the shift toward AI answer engines and the growing share of views coming from the home page and sidebar rather than search. Kyle's is from June 2026, and he explicitly describes HubSpot *moving away* from the older keyword-first approach.
2. **Size of the evidence base.** Kyle is describing a decision applied across roughly 13 channels at a company running YouTube for over five years, rather than one channel's experience.

**[my inference: the split between "searched for it" and "appeared in front of them" is mine. Neither source frames the disagreement this way, and neither acknowledges the other's case.]**

### Disagreement 2: Where to start in the funnel

**Kyle Denhoff recommends starting at the bottom.** He states it as a deliberate sequencing choice: "I always like to start at the bottom of the traditional funnel and work my way up because I want to prove the revenue impact so I can get more investment to keep going." Product tutorials are stage one, the educational layer comes second, thought leadership third (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026).

**Samu Kovács and Greg Miroslaw both lead with the top.** Samu's 40/30/30 allocates the largest share to top of funnel and his Calendly plan's central argument is that bottom-of-funnel-only content captures a fraction of the market (source: Samu Kovács, YouTube, 29.06.2026). Greg names top-of-funnel content as the first thing to create, with the goal of getting as much qualified traffic to the channel as possible (source: Greg Miroslaw, YouTube, 18.05.2026).

**Where I land: Kyle Denhoff, and I think this is the most consequential disagreement in the set.** Both are internally consistent, but they are optimising for different risks. Samu and Greg are optimising for channel growth. Kyle is optimising for the channel surviving long enough to grow, which is the binding constraint for an in-house team that has to justify budget. Bottom-of-funnel videos are also the cheapest to make well, since they are about a product you already know, need no trend research, and rank against far weaker competition. There is a visible incentive difference worth naming: Samu and Greg both sell YouTube agency services, where an engagement's value is tied to producing reach, while Kyle is an in-house operator who has to defend a line item internally. That does not make Samu and Greg wrong, but it makes Kyle's constraint the one that matches most readers of this playbook. **Practical synthesis:** ship four to six bottom-of-funnel videos first to establish attribution and prove the channel converts, then shift the mix toward Samu's 40/30/30 once you have evidence to point at.

### Disagreement 3: Video length

**Samu Kovács and Marlon Doll both recommend long form.** Samu's critique of Calendly is that their videos sit in the 1 to 5 minute range and should move to longer form (source: Samu Kovács, YouTube, 29.06.2026). Marlon Doll is blunter: "Don't settle for quick 2-minute explainers. If you want authority, aim for 10 plus minute deep dives that truly solve the problem and become the best video on the topic" (source: Marlon Doll, YouTube, 10.12.2025).

**Jake Trinder's own headline B2B result was a 2-minute video.** Describing how he got over 500,000 views on a B2B YouTube video, his stated method was to find a content gap and then "made a 2-minute video that answered the exact question. Delivered value fast, no long intro, no fluff, no wasted time" (source: Jake Trinder, LinkedIn, 19.07.2025 — https://www.linkedin.com/posts/jaketrinder_i-got-500k-views-on-a-b2b-youtube-video-share-7352357875035377665-ttO6/).

**Where I land: Marlon Doll's framing, with Jake's exception preserved, and I discount Samu's numbers.** The instruction that generalises is Marlon's underlying principle rather than his duration: be the best video on the topic and match the length the question deserves. A question with a 2-minute answer padded to 12 minutes will lose retention, which is the metric that actually drives distribution, so length is downstream of question scope rather than a target in itself. I note that Samu states the range two different ways in the same video, first as "90% of the best performing YouTube videos are between 8 and 50 minutes" and then as an 8 to 15 minute recommendation, which is a large enough discrepancy that I would not build a production standard on the figure. **Practical rule [my inference]:** let the question set the length, then check retention. If average view duration drops sharply at a consistent timestamp, the video was too long for its question, not too short for the algorithm.

### Disagreement 4: Research tooling, proprietary depth vs. commodity tools

**Jake Trinder argues the common tools give you no edge.** "A lot of these videos that you're going to find through applying this algorithm, you wouldn't find if you were to just use a tool like one of 10, view stats, vidIQ, etc. Plus, everybody has access to those tools. So you've got zero competitive advantage if you use those tools." His alternative is a manual depth-first search across niches, and he describes hundreds of hours of research a week, an in-house research team, and over $200,000 a month spent on his YouTube system (source: Jake Trinder, YouTube, 28.06.2026).

**Marlon Doll, Austin Schneider, and Sam Oh all use commodity tools.** Marlon: "We like to use Vid IQ and one of 10 Chrome extensions to spot outliers" (source: Marlon Doll, YouTube, 10.12.2025). Austin recommends vidIQ for title and description optimisation (source: Austin Schneider, "12 B2B Marketing Strategies For 2025", YouTube, 18.04.2025 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KtX1srhbC2k). Sam Oh uses Ahrefs Keywords Explorer, having tested and published the accuracy problems with the others (source: Sam Oh, YouTube, 27.07.2022).

**Where I land: take Jake's method, ignore Jake's scale, and keep using the cheap tools.**

The useful move here is to separate two things Jake presents as one package: *how he thinks about research*, which is excellent and free, and *how much he spends doing it*, which is irrelevant to you.

**His scale is not a method you can adopt.** Hundreds of research hours a week, a dedicated in-house research team, and over $200,000 a month is a description of an agency you could hire, not a process a marketing team of one to five people can run. He is straightforward about this in the video, where the research walkthrough leads into an offer to run your channel for you.

**His argument against common tools does not hold up.** He says that because vidIQ, 1of10, and ViewStats are cheap and widely available, using them gives you no competitive advantage. The flaw: a tool has never been the advantage. *Acting on what it shows you* is the advantage. Everyone in your category can see the same outlier data, and almost nobody does anything with it. This playbook contains two named illustrations of exactly that: Lemlist posting unstructured content while Instantly executes, and Calendly leaving obvious demand unserved. Neither company is losing because they lack a Chrome extension.

**He also does not follow his own rule.** Minutes after dismissing those tools, he reads outlier multipliers off the 1of10 extension and checks subscriber counts with vidIQ while demonstrating the process on camera. That is not disqualifying, but it tells you the tools are fine and the "no advantage" line is positioning.

**What is genuinely worth taking, and costs nothing:**

1. **Look outside your own niche for proven formats.** A video structure that works in an unrelated category, but has never been done in yours, is an opportunity your direct competitors cannot see because they only watch each other.
2. **Verify an outlier before you copy it.** Check the comment count against the view count and look at the view graph. A big number with very little engagement usually means traffic was pushed to that video, not that the audience genuinely rewarded it ([1.4](#phase-1-research-find-the-topics-before-you-script)).

That second habit is the sharpest thing in the entire research set, and every other source in this playbook treats high view counts as proof without ever testing for it.

### Disagreement 5: Who should be on camera

**TK Kader and Austin Schneider both argue for founder-led.** TK's entire framework is built on sharing your own journey, covering the problem you solve, lessons learned, and customer success stories, and he explicitly rejects ghostwriters and templated content as producing likes without revenue (source: TK Kader, YouTube, 18.05.2025, and LinkedIn, 18.05.2025). Austin cites 64% of consumers trusting founder content over corporate content and says raw, unscripted content outperforms cinematic production (source: Austin Schneider, YouTube, 18.04.2025).

**Greg Miroslaw lays out three staffing models with explicit trade-offs and does not treat founder-led as automatically correct.** Founder or team member gives the highest trust, authenticity, and lowest cost, but demands founder time on camera and is hard to scale or transition. A hired presenter frees founder time, allows higher volume without burnout, and gives professional delivery from day one, at the cost of authenticity, ongoing expense, and rebuilding audience trust if that person leaves. Creator partnerships give instant access to warm audiences with no long-term commitment, but you do not own the audience, have limited brand control, and it does not compound. He names Clay and Instantly as channels using a dedicated face rather than the founder (source: Greg Miroslaw, YouTube, 18.05.2026).

**Where I land: Greg Miroslaw. You are asking the wrong question if you ask "should the founder be on camera?"**

The better question is:

> **Who will still be recording these videos in 12 months?**

**Why "founder-led" is not the principle it gets treated as.** TK Kader presents founder-led content as close to a moral requirement, and this is the weakest part of an otherwise strong framework. Greg supplies the disproof by name: Clay and Instantly both run successful channels using a dedicated presenter rather than the founder. So a founder on camera is clearly not required for a B2B SaaS channel to work. Greg is also the only source who treats this as a staffing decision with real trade-offs on both sides, instead of a matter of authenticity.

**What actually matters is that the same person keeps showing up.** YouTube rewards consistency of talent, format, and schedule ([2.4](#phase-2-content-architecture-the-three-layers)). Consistency is a property of a *person continuing to appear*, not of that person's job title. **A founder who quits after five videos is worse for the channel than a hired presenter who lasts three years.** Kyle Denhoff removes the usual objection from the other direction: you do not need a charismatic, loud host, just someone educational with real lived experience (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026 — https://youtu.be/6TBAHC7ksbU).

**So decide it this way:**

| Your situation | Do this |
|---|---|
| Founder genuinely commits to 12 months of recording | **Founder-led.** Highest trust, lowest cost, and it compounds. |
| Founder has the expertise but dislikes being on camera | **Interview format.** A host asks the questions and carries the performance, so the founder only has to talk about what they already know. This is Kyle Denhoff's suggestion and the best hedge available. |
| Founder cannot commit the time, or is planning to step back | **Hire a presenter, or use a senior team member who will stay.** Accept lower authenticity in exchange for a channel that survives. |
| You are tempted to start founder-led "and see how it goes" | **Do not.** Switching faces later means rebuilding audience trust, which Greg names as the main risk of the presenter model. Choose based on the 12-month answer, not on enthusiasm in week one. |

## What I rejected and why

### 1. Matt Gray's content waterfall as a starting point

**The idea:** take one pillar YouTube video and turn it into 26+ pieces across Instagram Reels, TikTok, Shorts, LinkedIn posts, X threads, a newsletter, and podcast platforms (source: Matt Gray, "copy this content strategy, it'll blow up your business", YouTube, 12.09.2025 — https://youtu.be/nJhTQpiuNow, and LinkedIn, 13.01.2026 — https://www.linkedin.com/posts/mattgray1_quality-over-quantity-ugcPost-7416809306010066945-E5oT/).

**Why I rejected it for this playbook:** it optimises distribution before the source content has been validated. If the pillar video has poor retention, the waterfall reliably manufactures 26 pieces of content nobody wanted, at 26 times the production overhead. It also contradicts the strongest advice in the set from two directions: TK Kader's pick-one-channel principle (source: TK Kader, YouTube, 18.05.2025) and Kyle Denhoff's warning that trying to be everywhere is unsustainable and that you should narrow to two or three channels where your audience actually is (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 11.11.2024). It is also the one recommendation here whose evidence base is a creator-economy channel rather than a B2B SaaS company. **What I kept:** repurposing is worth doing *after* a video demonstrates retention, as a way to extend the reach of something already proven to work.

### 2. Austin Schneider's in-niche research method

**The idea:** "go on YouTube and research people who are talking about what it is that you offer. And then from there, I would pretty much try to repurpose those videos and make them my own" (source: Austin Schneider, YouTube, 18.04.2025 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KtX1srhbC2k).

**A note on the wording first, in fairness to him.** "Repurpose those videos and make them my own" sounds worse than it is. Seconds earlier he says "You're the subject matter expert" and tells you to film your own training and guides, so in context he means ordinary topic research, not remaking someone else's video. I am not accusing him of advocating plagiarism. My objection is to the method, not his integrity.

**Why I rejected it: researching your own niche is a parity strategy. It cannot, by construction, make you different from your competitors.**

Austin tells you to research "people who are talking about what it is that you offer". That is your own category, and it is the single worst place to look, for a reason that has nothing to do with effort or execution: **every competitor you have is running that exact search.** They are typing the same terms into YouTube and watching the same top results. Anything you discover there, they have already discovered. The best possible outcome of that method is a slightly better version of a video your category has already seen, which is parity, not advantage. Do it well and you tie.

**The counter-evidence is sitting inside this research set.** The format behind Instantly's roughly 440,000-view video did not come from the cold email niche. Jake Trinder took it from a creator selling digital products, an entirely unrelated category, and it worked *because* nobody in cold email had done it before (source: Jake Trinder, YouTube, 28.06.2026 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LOgDwuUsmzw). Follow Austin's instruction and research only cold email videos, and that idea is invisible to you. Marlon Doll independently says the same thing: study not only your business competitors but "competitors of your target audience's attention" (source: Marlon Doll, YouTube, 10.12.2025 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OBK5DZJZnjs).

Worth noting where that leaves the advice: the very channel this video is published on owes one of its biggest wins to the opposite method.

**What I use instead ([1.3](#phase-1-research-find-the-topics-before-you-script)):**

- ❌ **Search inside your niche.** You search "cold email tips", find the three best videos, make your own version. So did everyone else selling cold email software. **Ceiling: parity.**
- ✅ **Search adjacent niches.** Find a format proven in a category whose viewers behave like yours, but which nobody in your category has used yet, then fill it with your product, your data, your result. **Ceiling: something your category has never seen.**

**Two rules, and you need both.** Borrow the container, never what goes inside it. And go looking for that container somewhere your competitors are not looking, because a format everyone in your niche can see is not an advantage, it is a commodity.

### 3. Paid creator partnerships as an early-stage growth lever

**The idea:** pay creators in your space for videos or YouTube-native partner videos to borrow their audience and trust while you have none (source: Greg Miroslaw, YouTube, 18.05.2026, who presents this as his "best hack").

**Why I rejected it for the default playbook:** by Greg's own later analysis in the same video, the audience does not compound, you do not own it, brand control is limited, and it gets expensive at scale. Spending budget to rent attention before you know whether your content converts means you learn nothing transferable when it works. It also puts a cash cost in front of the cheapest available proof, which is bottom-of-funnel content aimed at demand you already have. **When I would reconsider:** after the channel has proven conversion, as an accelerant rather than a foundation, which is roughly how Greg himself frames the mature case.

## My original ideas

### Original idea 1: Let your lost deals pick your next video

**The idea in one sentence.** Write down the questions prospects ask in sales calls, then make videos about the ones that keep losing you deals.

**How it works.** One shared spreadsheet. After every sales call, the rep adds a row or ticks an existing one. Once a month, marketing sorts it and takes the top row as the next video.

| Question the prospect asked | Times asked | Deals lost after it came up | Score (asked × lost) |
|---|---|---|---|
| "How are you different from [competitor]?" | 14 | 9 | **126** |
| "Does it integrate with our CRM?" | 22 | 3 | 66 |
| "How long does onboarding take?" | 11 | 4 | 44 |
| "Is our data secure?" | 8 | 2 | 16 |

In this example your next video is a comparison against that competitor. Notice what the score protects you from: the CRM question gets asked most often, so it would win on frequency alone, but people ask it and buy anyway. The competitor question comes up less and costs you far more. **Popular is not the same as expensive.**

**Why this is not in the sources.** All ten pick topics by what is *popular*: search volume (Sam Oh, Greg Miroslaw), high view counts in the niche (Jake Trinder, Marlon Doll), or trends. Kyle Denhoff gets closest, saying research should include listening to sales calls, but he treats that as one input among many, not as the thing that decides what you film next (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026 — https://youtu.be/6TBAHC7ksbU). Nobody links video choice to revenue you are actually losing.

**Why it could work.** Three reasons:

1. **It gives you a budget argument.** "This video targets the objection that appeared in nine lost deals last quarter" survives a review meeting. "This keyword gets 2,000 searches a month" does not.
2. **Competitors cannot copy it.** These patterns come out of your own pipeline. Nobody else can see your lost deals, so the resulting video is automatically differentiated.
3. **It matches how buyers now search.** Kyle Denhoff expects buyers to increasingly ask AI tools about pricing, features, and comparisons before talking to sales (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026). This spreadsheet is literally a ranked list of those questions.

**How to check it worked.** Take deals where that objection came up. Compare the win rate for prospects who watched the video against those who did not.

**Where it falls short.** This only produces middle and bottom-of-funnel videos, so it cannot grow your reach on its own and has to run alongside the 40/30/30 mix ([2.1](#phase-2-content-architecture-the-three-layers)). It also dies quietly if sales stop filling in the sheet, which is the most likely way it fails.

### Original idea 2: Decide what failure looks like before you publish video one

**The idea in one sentence.** Before you upload anything, write down the numbers that would tell you the *format* is wrong, so that "keep going" never becomes the only possible answer.

**Write down something like this, and date it:**

> After **12** videos published on schedule, we review. If the typical video holds under **25%** of its audience (average view duration) and under **3%** of people who see the thumbnail click it (click-through rate), we change the format instead of publishing 12 more.

**Why this is not in the sources.** Every source gives you a commitment but never an exit. Greg Miroslaw says one video a week for 90 days before judging (source: Greg Miroslaw, YouTube, 18.05.2026 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gZZ5D1bceMA). Kyle Denhoff says one every two weeks for three months, then see how it goes (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026). Neither says what "it went badly" actually looks like. So when 90 days pass and nothing has happened, the only advice left is "be more consistent", and that can run forever.

**Why it could work.** A channel can underperform for two completely different reasons, and the correct response to each is the opposite of the other:

| Why it is not working | What you should do |
|---|---|
| It has not run long enough yet | Keep going. Same format, same schedule. |
| The format is wrong | Change it. Twelve more videos will not fix it. |

Without a number agreed in advance, you cannot tell these two apart, and you will pick whichever one you prefer to believe. That gets harder to resist once someone has personally been on camera a dozen times.

**Where it falls short.** I chose 25% and 3% myself. They come from the two metrics Kyle Denhoff names as the main signals YouTube reacts to (source: Kyle Denhoff, YouTube, 23.06.2026), not from published benchmarks, and the right numbers will differ by industry and video length. **Treat the numbers as an example. The recommendation is the habit of agreeing them before you start, not these specific figures.**

## Weaknesses of this playbook

Every playbook has weak spots. Most do not tell you where they are. Here are mine, grouped into three types, each with a line on what it should change about how much you trust the advice.

> **If you read only one thing here:** almost everyone I learned from sells YouTube services for a living, and I never saw a single company that followed this advice and still failed. Those two facts, together, mean this playbook probably makes YouTube look like a safer bet than it is.

### A. Problems with the people I learned from

**1. Most of them are selling something.** Six of the ten make money from you believing this works. Samu Kovács (KS Media), Greg Miroslaw, Marlon Doll (Vireo Video), and Jake Trinder all sell YouTube agency or coaching services, and pitch them inside the very videos I cited. TK Kader pitches a coaching programme. Austin Schneider's video is essentially a long advert for Instantly, on whose channel it appears.
→ **What this means for you:** nobody in this group has any reason to tell you YouTube is a poor fit for your company. Kyle Denhoff and Sam Oh are the least conflicted, and it is not an accident that I ended up siding with Kyle in three of the five disagreements.

**2. I only ever saw the winners.** Every example here is a channel that worked: Calendly's proposed fix, GitHub, Instantly, Glenoko, Ahrefs. Not one source discusses a company that did all of this properly and still got nowhere.
→ **What this means for you:** I can tell you what successful channels did. I cannot tell you whether unsuccessful channels did exactly the same things. Those are different questions, and only the second one tells you your odds.

**3. The impressive numbers cannot be checked.** "$1 million in 6 months", "over $2 million a year", "$320M/yr", "$300,000 a month". Every one of these is a figure quoted by the person who benefits from you believing it, in a video selling their service.
→ **What this means for you:** I have written these as claims rather than facts throughout. There is no independently verified financial result anywhere in this playbook.

**4. Most experts gave me only one video each.** Six of the ten are represented by a single video, and four had no relevant LinkedIn posts at all. One video is one argument on one day, not someone's full position.
→ **What this means for you:** I may have flattened views that are more nuanced across their wider work. Greg Miroslaw's LinkedIn citation also points at his profile rather than a permanent post link, which is a genuine gap in the trail.

### B. Problems with the raw material

**5. Some of it is old.** Sam Oh's core SEO video is from July 2022, roughly four years before this was written, and predates both AI search and the shift toward home-page and sidebar traffic. Kyle Denhoff's Haley Marketing appearance is from November 2024.
→ **What this means for you:** I flagged this where it changes a recommendation ([Disagreement 1](#disagreement-1-keyword-first-titles-vs-editorial-hooks)), but smaller tactical details are probably out of date in ways I cannot detect from a transcript alone.

**6. The transcripts contain errors.** These are auto-generated captions. "Calendly" shows up as "Kalanley", "Canonly", and "Candy". TK Kader's company name and URL are both mangled.
→ **What this means for you:** I read around the obvious errors, but auto-captions can occasionally flip a meaning, and I did not check every quoted phrase against the original audio.

### C. What this playbook does not cover

**7. It is written for smaller SaaS, not enterprise.** Nearly all the evidence comes from SMB and product-led companies with short, self-serve sales cycles. There is essentially nothing about enterprise SaaS: six-figure contracts, buying committees of six to ten people, and 9 to 18 month cycles, where YouTube's role is probably different in kind.
→ **What this means for you:** if you sell to enterprise, treat this as a starting hypothesis, not a procedure. I am guessing there, and guessing is not what the rest of this document is doing.

**8. There is no cost information anywhere.** Not one source says what any of this costs: filming, editing, thumbnails, a presenter, or internal hours.
→ **What this means for you:** "publish weekly for 90 days" is a budget decision, and this playbook cannot tell you whether that budget is small or large. That makes it hard to compare fairly against the paid advertising alternative that Kyle himself points to.

**9. The AI search section will date fastest.** AEO is genuinely new, the headline statistic comes from one vendor selling the tool that measures it, and these systems change faster than any of these videos.
→ **What this means for you:** trust Phase 6 least, and re-check it before acting on it.

**10. Topics nobody covered at all.** Legal or compliance review for regulated industries. Non-English markets. Accessibility beyond captions used as an SEO trick. What to do with a video that goes out of date after a product redesign. Hiring, beyond one rough sketch from Matt Gray. And notably: what happens to a founder-led channel when that founder leaves the company. Greg raises exactly that risk for hired presenters, but nobody applies it to founders.

## Who I would NOT recommend following, and why

To be fair to everyone here: all ten met the bar for inclusion, and none is a fraud. This section is about who I would not point a B2B SaaS marketer to *for this specific topic*.

### 1. Matt Gray, for this topic

**Why not.** His content is general founder and creator-economy content strategy, and the evidence he offers is his own newsletter and personal channel growth, not a B2B SaaS outcome. His central prescription, the 26-piece content waterfall, is the one recommendation in the set that directly contradicts the two most credible sources on channel focus (see [What I rejected, 1](#1-matt-grays-content-waterfall-as-a-starting-point)), and it is volume-first advice offered under a "quality over quantity" headline.

There is also a specific accuracy problem that changed how much weight I gave him. In the same LinkedIn post, he introduces "The Content Quality Checklist" with "run it through these 5 checks" and then lists four (source: Matt Gray, LinkedIn, 13.01.2026). It is a small error, but the entire value proposition of a named proprietary framework is that it has been used enough times to be reliable, and a framework whose author miscounts its own steps in a post about quality control has probably not been stress-tested much. Combined with the video functioning largely as a funnel into his paid product, I would send a B2B SaaS marketer to Samu Kovács or Marlon Doll for the same funnel concepts, better evidenced and actually about SaaS.

**Where he is still worth reading:** solo founders building a personal brand across platforms, which is genuinely his domain.

### 2. Austin Schneider, for this topic

**Why not.** Three reasons, in increasing order of seriousness. First, relevance: YouTube is item 4 of 12 in his video, and his treatment of it is roughly two minutes of general encouragement, so the topical yield is thin. Second, an internal factual inconsistency: within one segment he cites 64% of executives using Meta and then 68% of executives using it, and none of his statistics carry a named source beyond one attribution to Salesforce (source: Austin Schneider, YouTube, 18.04.2025). Third, and most substantively, the research method he prescribes looks only inside your own niche, which caps you at parity with every competitor running the same searches, and is contradicted by two other sources in this set (see [What I rejected, 2](#2-austin-schneiders-in-niche-research-method)). His phrasing there is also loose enough that a beginner could read it as remaking a competitor's video, though the surrounding context indicates he does not mean that.

The conflict of interest is also the sharpest in the set. The video is published on Instantly's own channel, and the strategy he advocates most strongly and at greatest length is cold email, which is Instantly's product, with an extended walkthrough of the tool. That does not make the cold email advice wrong, and Instantly's channel is genuinely a strong example of B2B SaaS YouTube done well, which is why Greg Miroslaw uses it as his positive case. But it makes this particular video an advertisement first and a strategy resource second.

**Where he is still worth reading:** as a *specimen* rather than a *source*. Instantly's channel is one of the best-executed B2B SaaS YouTube channels in this research, and studying what they do is more instructive than this video about what they do.

### 3. Jake Trinder, with a caveat rather than a rejection

I stop short of a full "do not follow" here, because his outlier-verification method ([1.4](#phase-1-research-find-the-topics-before-you-script)) is the sharpest single piece of methodology I found. But his headline prescription is unusable at the scale most readers operate at, he dismisses tools he then uses on camera minutes later, and the video's function is to sell a done-for-you service and an inner circle. Take the method, ignore the scale.
